The method
  1. Assume every diode is cut off. Replace each one with an open circuit.
  2. Find VP − VN across each diode in that open-circuit version of the circuit.
  3. If VP − VN > UD, the assumption contradicts itself. Reject it and replace that diode with its conducting model: a voltage source UD, + at the anode.
    With several diodes, reject the one with the largest discrepancy first, then go back to step 2.
  4. Sanity check. A reverse voltage beyond the breakdown voltage on an ordinary diode means the analysis is probably wrong.
  1. Zener working in a regulator, connected in reverse: a voltage source UZ, + at the cathode.
  2. Diodes in parallel: UD takes priority over UZ. Within the same type, the smaller value wins.
Part 1

Two states, two models

The whole method is a choice between two replacements for each diode. Pick one, solve a linear circuit, and check whether the choice was consistent.

Fine print

Some textbooks separate the turn-on voltage Uon (about 0.5 V for Si, where current starts) from the drop UD (about 0.7 V, while conducting). In the constant-voltage-drop model used in most exam problems both are taken as the same number, so your test VP − VN > UD is the standard one. With the ideal-diode model, UD = 0 and the test becomes simply VP > VN.

Part 2

One diode: assume, contradict, replace

Example 1 · find VA and ID
  • Assume
    D is open, so no current flows through it and A is just the midpoint of a divider. VA = 10 × 3 / (2 + 3) = 6 V
  • Check
    VP − VN = 6 − 4 = 2 V, which is more than 0.7 V. contradiction
  • Replace
    D conducts. Put a 0.7 V source in its place, + at the anode. VA = 4 + 0.7 = 4.7 V
  • Verify
    IR1 = (10 − 4.7) / 2 kΩ = 2.65 mAIR2 = 4.7 / 3 kΩ = 1.57 mAID = 2.65 − 1.57 = 1.08 mA > 0Current flows from P to N. consistent
  • Answer
    VA = 4.7 V, ID ≈ 1.08 mA
Fine print: the second check

Your method tests the open assumption. The conducting assumption has a matching test: once the diode is replaced by its source, the current through it must flow from P to N (ID > 0). If it comes out negative, the "on" choice contradicts itself in turn and the diode is really off. In exam circuits this check almost always passes, but it costs one line and catches mistakes.

Part 3

Several diodes: largest discrepancy first

Assume all diodes are off. Of the ones that contradict, switch on only the one with the largest VP − VN. Then recompute everything and repeat.

Example 2 · find VO
  • Assume
    Both open. No current in R, so VO = −6 V.
  • Check
    D1: 6 − (−6) = 12 VD2: 2 − (−6) = 8 VBoth exceed 0.7 V. D1's discrepancy is larger. reject D1 first
  • Replace
    D1 becomes a 0.7 V source. VO = 6 − 0.7 = 5.3 V
  • Re-check
    D2 in the new circuit: 2 − 5.3 = −3.3 VD2 is now reverse biased, so it stays open. consistent
  • Answer
    VO = 5.3 V, D1 on, D2 off, I = 11.3 mA

Why not start with D2? If D2 conducts, VO = 1.3 V, which puts 6 − 1.3 = 4.7 V across D1. A diode cannot sit above 0.7 V and stay off, so you would have to back up and switch D1 on anyway. Starting from the largest discrepancy skips that detour.

Fine print

"Largest first" is a search strategy, not a law of circuits. It works reliably when the diodes share a node, as in OR/AND gates and clippers, which is what most problems use. The part that makes it safe is the re-check: after each replacement, recompute every remaining diode in the new circuit, because one that looked forward biased can flip, as D2 did here. If a later step ever contradicts an earlier choice, undo that choice and try the next diode.

Part 4

Breakdown sanity check

When your answer leaves an ordinary diode reverse biased, look at how large that reverse voltage is. If it exceeds the breakdown voltage UBR, the diode could not stay cut off. Exam circuits are almost never designed that way, so treat it as a sign of an error: a dropped minus sign, the wrong reference node, or diodes switched on in the wrong order.

In Example 2, D2 sits at 3.3 V reverse. Any ordinary diode handles that easily, so the answer passes.

Fine print

This check is for ordinary diodes. A Zener is built to work in reverse breakdown, so for a Zener, reaching UZ is the normal operating case covered next, not a warning sign.

Part 5

Zener: a voltage source in reverse

The I–V curve has two steep walls. Near either wall, the voltage barely moves while the current changes a lot. That is exactly what a voltage source does, which is why each wall becomes a source in the model.

The forward wall gives UD. The reverse wall gives UZ, with + at the cathode.

The method is the same. Assume the Zener is open. If the reverse voltage across it, VN − VP, is larger than UZ, reject that and put a UZ source in its place.

Example 3 · find VO for two loads
  • a. Assume
    Zener open, so R and RL form a divider. VO = 12 × 2 / (1 + 2) = 8 V
  • Check
    8 V reverse is more than UZ = 6 V. contradiction
  • Replace
    VO = 6 V. IR = (12 − 6) / 1 kΩ = 6 mAIL = 6 / 2 kΩ = 3 mAIZ = 6 − 3 = 3 mA > 0 consistent
  • b. Assume
    Zener open. VO = 12 × 0.5 / (1 + 0.5) = 4 V
  • Check
    4 V is less than 6 V, so the open assumption holds. no contradiction
  • Answer
    a: VO = 6 V, IZ = 3 mA. b: VO = 4 V, Zener off.
Fine print: what "working normally" means

A Zener regulates only while its reverse current stays in range: IZmin ≤ IZ ≤ IZmax. Case b is the extreme low end, where the load takes so much current that the Zener drops out. If a question gives IZmax or the power rating PZM, check IZ against it too. And a Zener connected forward is not a UZ source at all: it behaves like an ordinary diode with UD.

Part 6

Diodes in parallel: the priority rule

UD takes priority over UZ. Within the same type, the smaller value wins.

Each element, once it conducts, pins the voltage across it at its own value. The element with the lowest clamp voltage reaches its threshold first and holds the node there. Every other branch then sees less than it needs and stays open. UD (about 0.7 V) is smaller than any practical UZ, which is why the ordinary diode wins.

Example 4 · find VO
  • Assume
    Both open. No current in R, so VO = 10 V.
  • Check
    D needs 0.7 V, the Zener needs 5 V. Both are exceeded, by 9.3 V and 5 V. D first
  • Replace
    D becomes a 0.7 V source, so VO = 0.7 V.
  • Re-check
    The Zener now sees only 0.7 V reverse, less than 5 V. It stays open. consistent
  • Answer
    VO = 0.7 V, ID = (10 − 0.7) / 1 kΩ = 9.3 mA

Fine print: when the rule applies

The rule is a shortcut for "the smallest clamp voltage wins," and it holds when three things are true. The branches connect the same two nodes. They would conduct for the same polarity, so a branch that conducts the other way is not competing. And no branch has its own series resistor; with one, fall back to the full assume-and-check method.

It also agrees with your several-diodes rule. All parallel branches see the same open-circuit voltage, so the branch with the smallest clamp voltage is exactly the one with the largest discrepancy.

Check yourself

Self-test

A single Si diode shows VP − VN = 0.4 V with the open assumption. What state is it in?

Cut off. 0.4 V is below UD ≈ 0.7 V, so there is no contradiction and the open circuit stands.

In Example 2, change the +2 V source to +8 V. What is VO?

All open: D1 sees 12 V, D2 sees 14 V, so D2 goes first. VO = 8 − 0.7 = 7.3 V. Re-check D1: 6 − 7.3 = −1.3 V, reverse, so D1 is off. VO = 7.3 V.

A 6.2 V Zener, connected in reverse, sees 5 V reverse with the open assumption. Model?

Open circuit. 5 V is below UZ, so it has not broken down.

A Zener is connected forward and the open test gives VP − VN = 3 V. Model?

A UD source (about 0.7 V), + at the anode, the same as an ordinary diode. UZ only applies in reverse.

A 3.3 V and a 4.7 V Zener, both reverse, in parallel, fed from +12 V through a resistor. VO?

3.3 V. Same type, so the smaller value wins and the 4.7 V Zener stays off.

Your answer puts 150 V reverse across a diode rated UBR = 100 V. What now?

Go back over the work. A reverse voltage beyond breakdown means the diode could not be cut off, and exam circuits are not designed that way. Look for a sign error or a wrongly chosen first diode.

Figures use the constant-voltage-drop model with UD = 0.7 V.
